        How the baskets are built

        Members come from IBKR's theme lists and the night's research, and each one is checked first: a US listing and at least about $20M a day of average trading over 90 days. Today's moves come from daily bars, or the stored snapshot before bars exist. The 5- and 20-day spread and its z-score come from daily bars, rebalanced to equal weight each day, and appear once every member has bars.

        The hedge is one dollar against one dollar, with no beta adjustment, so baskets that swing more than their ETF (quantum, neoclouds, space) partly measure the market's direction. Members are chosen for today's story, so their past spreads look cleaner than anything tradable at the time.

        How the swing book works

        The swing book trades on an IBKR paper account, never a live one. It starts from the nightly screen: a research pass judges every candidate on it and writes a verdict, take or pass, with a conviction from 1 to 5 and its reasoning. Only the takes are traded, up to 5 a session, strongest conviction first, at $10,000 a side. Each take carries the plan the pass wrote for it: a time limit of up to 10 sessions and a stop between 1% and 8%. A name already in an open automatic trade isn't taken again.

        The research pass runs three times a day. In the evening it writes the verdicts for the next open. Before the open it lets each take stand or stands it down on what happened overnight. Before the close it reviews every open automatic trade and can close one early for a stated reason, or ask for a winner on its last session to run up to 3 sessions longer, once. It can never widen a stop or hold a loser past its time limit. Every verdict, taken or passed, is scored against its own plan on daily bars, so the passes are the control for the takes.

        Entries go into the opening auction as market-on-open orders sent at 9:15 AM ET. At 9:40 AM ET the fills are checked: a trade with one side filled and the other not has its filled side closed at the market. At 3:45 PM ET every open trade is checked against its stop and time limit on current prices, and the ones leaving are sent as market-on-close orders. A pair whose other leg never filled leaves at that day's close. Caps: 60 open trades, $900,000 gross and $30,000 net in any one symbol.

        How the intraday book works

        A second book, separate from the swing book, that trades the same ideas on a 15-minute clock. Haiku’s takes are traded on the paper account, $10,000 a side, with market orders; its passes are only followed. A minute after each 15-minute bar closes, from 10:00 AM to 3:45 PM ET, it checks the liquid names for four setups, each measured against the name’s hedge fund: a gap that holds, a gap that fades, a break of the first 30 minutes’ range on heavy volume, and a stretched run that turns back.

        Names that reported earnings since the last close and names the swing book holds are skipped. The four heaviest-volume signals at each bar go to Claude Haiku with the name’s recent Yahoo headlines for a take or a pass, up to five takes a day and three open at once.

        Every trade is a pair in equal dollars. It opens at the next bar’s open, stops at a loss of half the name’s daily ATR, takes a gain of one ATR, and is out by the 3:45 PM bar. A stop is seen at a bar’s close and filled at the next open, so losses can run past it. Costs are assumed at 8 bp a trade.

        Hormuz

        What prices the strait

        Dubai crude is the benchmark Gulf barrels are sold on, and JKM is the Asian LNG price that most Qatari cargoes follow. Brent and TTF show how far the rest of the world moves with them.

        Crossings through the strait, last 30 days

        —

        Ships crossing in either direction, by what they carry, from the live AIS tracker at hormuz.data-tracking.net, polled every 30 minutes. Today’s bar is the day so far. It counts more ships than the PortWatch chart beside it because it defines a crossing differently: compare each with itself, not with the other.

        Tankers through the strait, per day

        Every tanker transit that satellite tracking saw, as a 7-day average, with single days behind it.

        Live crossings come from hormuz.data-tracking.net (CC BY 4.0), which polls ship transponders every 30 minutes; its source and coverage aren’t published. The map’s state and the tanker history come from IMF PortWatch, which updates daily with about two days’ lag. PortWatch counts tankers and their cargo capacity, not barrels: the oil and LNG figures scale the U.S. EIA’s 2024 flows by tanker capacity against the 2025 average, and are estimates. Ships that switch off their transponders are not counted. Lanes and terminals are drawn for orientation. Pipeline figures are capacity, not live flow.

        How the chart is built

        Prices come from Yahoo Finance when you open a name, and nothing is saved. Pre-market and after-hours bars show as gray candles on shaded bands with only their open and close, since Yahoo gives no volume there and its highs and lows can be bad prints. The price scale fits the candles, so an average far from price can sit off the chart; its value stays in the key. RSI 14, ATR 14 and the dashed averages use regular-session 15-minute bars, so a 200-bar average covers about 15 sessions. The solid lines are the 20, 50 and 200-day averages on dividend-adjusted prices, and the daily figures on the right match the nightly brief with today's session so far included.
